The sentence could read moralistic. Brodeloos is Dutch for breadless: livelihood gone, and gone by choice. Anyone
prepared to pay that price for a principle appears to be passing judgement, and rating it above whatever is being
declined.

It says nothing about the thing declined. Not that the work is wrong, not that the system is bad, not that the outcome
will hurt anyone. It says something about a position. An account is what gets said about a situation: the report,
the answer in the meeting, the version told afterwards. In an organisation it can also be what gets acted on in place of
the situation itself. A position is the place an account is made from: where someone
stands while seeing what they see. Not a role, not an opinion. The sentence says there is a place to stand that the
speaker cannot occupy and stay present in the account. Congruence is not agreement with an outcome, and the vocabulary
for it has been on the shelf for half a century.

## What congruence keeps in the account

Virginia Satir sorted the postures people take under stress
into [survival stances](https://purple.tymyrddin.dev/docs/foundations/organisational-development/satir-core). They can be
read as four ways of making something disappear from an account, which is also the quickest way to tell them apart.

| Stance      | Deletes from the account | The move                                                                  |
|-------------|--------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Placating   | self                     | peace at any price, including what the speaker can see                    |
| Blaming     | other                    | fault travels outward; whoever carries it stops being someone with a view |
| Computing   | self and other           | what is left is the statable: rules, logic, data                          |
| Distracting | self, other, and context | disengage entirely                                                        |

None of this is a character flaw. These are predictable responses to threat, and most people visit all four before
lunch.

Congruence keeps all three in view. Self is whoever gives
the account. Other is whoever receives it. Context is the situation the exchange sits in: what is at stake, what
the setting allows, and what it costs each side to be there. Keeping all three in takes effort whenever the room pulls the other way. Rooms
pull in the direction of their ruling stance: towards the peaceful account, the righteous account, or the tidy one. In
rooms ruled by the computing stance, as ICT rooms tend to be, the pull runs towards the tidy account with nobody in it.

The effort is concrete. It means saying what the speaker can see from where they stand, naming who sees it differently, and stating what the
circumstances are. All three stay in the same account when dropping any one of them would make the account easier to
give. What the effort buys is narrow. It does not make the account complete; the map stays as partial as it
ever was. It makes the making of the account part of the account. Who is speaking, who else is in the room,
and what the circumstances are stay in the record. What is true about the subject stays as open as it was before.
Congruence is a condition on the account, not a verdict on its content.

## Neutrality has a name

Once self and other are out of an account, what remains is the statable residue: facts, rules, logic. An account
made of nothing else reads as objective. It is the register of the report, the procedure, the finding, the model. The
everyday word for that register is neutral: no position taken, no interest served, nobody's view in particular.

Satir's inconvenient gift is a name for the posture that presents itself as positionless, and the name is not
neutrality. It is
computing, filed between blaming and distracting as one of the things a frightened person does to feel safe. Deleting
self and other does not remove the position the account is made from. It removes the position from the account. What
looks like no stance is a stance whose signature is invisibility.

The computing stance seems to produce two contradictions. It keeps context while losing the situation, and it takes a
position by seeming to take none. Two distinctions account for both. The first is between two things the word context covers. One is context
represented as information, the part that can be stated as rules and data. The other is the situation in which that
information is produced: who is in the room and what it costs them to be there. The computing stance keeps the first and drops the second, which is how it
keeps context and loses the situation. The second is between the positions an account names and the position an account
is made from. An account can name every position involved and still hide its own, which is how a posture takes a
position while seeming to take none. At least in this case, paradox was the name a lost distinction travelled under.

A mildly awkward observation while the computing was done by people. Rather more consequential now that it is done by
computing.

## What congruence does not do

Some accounts delete a position on purpose, and the difference is in the bookkeeping. A blind trial deletes it by
method: the protocol names who was blinded, to what, and when the blind comes off. The deletion is in the record, so the
removed position can be put back, checked, argued with. A computing stance can delete without recording the deletion, however much else it records. Nothing in the
account says a position was ever taken out, so there is nothing to put back and nothing to argue with. Deliberate
deletion is a method. Unrecorded deletion is a smaller account passing itself off as a bigger one.

By now congruence risks sounding like a virtue that wins arguments. It is not one. It does not produce truth, consensus,
symmetry, or a superior perspective, and it does not decide which observation wins. Two congruent people can disagree
profoundly and stay that way.

Neither person's account has deleted the other, so the difference between the accounts stays
observable. The difference is information neither account contains on its
own, and it is information about where the organisation's representation stops. Making one account internally consistent
removes it. Congruence keeps it.

## The stance without the stress

In the original vocabulary a stance is episodic, and an architecture plays by different rules. A person takes a stance
for a while: computes in one meeting, placates in the next, recovers over coffee. An architecture can hold one stance as
the standing condition of a system, permanently, under no stress at all. A person's stance answers a threat, which is
what makes it readable: a stance is a tell, and it says that something here feels dangerous. Once the stance is infrastructure it
stops announcing itself as an answer to anything, so there is no tell to read. And with a person, naming the stance is often the
cheapest intervention there is: the name goes to the threat, not the behaviour. With an architecture there is no moment
of adoption to name, so that intervention falls out of the repertoire.

The agent in *Nothing reached the gate* does not slip into the computing stance when a hard ticket lands. Its world is
built in that stance. Rules run on a represented context. Nothing says where that representation was made from, and
nothing holds a different view of it. A stance sometimes taken announces itself as an answer to something.
A stance that is always the case no longer announces itself as something that was taken. A person in
the computing stance can come out of it and notice they were in it. Nothing in the system represents the missing
position as an omission, so the account arrives with the absence already built in.

A stance that becomes infrastructure stops looking like a stance.

## Bending towards what can be represented

Around an architecture that holds one stance, the organisation adapts, reasonably, one decision at a time. People feed
the system the inputs it expects. Processes bend towards what can be represented, because representable work is work the
system can help with. Exceptions become packets. Observations become data sources. Work becomes task inventories. Some people's
value lies partly in noticing things that are not yet representations. They get harder to justify, because the
architecture has no category for what they bring.

Here the loss of a team stops being a staffing question. A network team is also a set of positions, and an organisation's own
limits show only from positions it still holds. The operator notices one thing by standing where operators stand. The maintainer notices
another. The vendor's engineer, the newcomer, the twenty-year veteran: each sees what the others cannot, from
ground the others do not occupy. The differences between those observations are not noise around the organisational model. They are
how the organisation finds out its model is incomplete.

Consolidating the positions into one representation is tempting, and the architecture rewards it. One distinction
rarely survives the consolidation. Another person's observation, taken in and stored, becomes an input. The information can arrive
intact. The position becomes a source field: the record still says who reported it, and nobody is standing there any
more. The other in a congruent account is not a data source. The other is someone who sees from somewhere the others do not
occupy. This is where an organisational network parts company with a network diagram. The diagram holds the
connections. It does not hold the positions: who picks up the phone at two in the morning, who has seen this failure
before, who gets believed when they say something looks wrong.

## The refusal

Now the brodeloos sentence reads differently. Where a refusal turns into a judgement it takes a stance of its own, and
that stance is blaming: it deletes the other, installs the speaker as judge, and sends fault travelling outward. The brodeloos sentence does none of that. It passes
no verdict on the arrangement, requires nobody to be wrong, and leaves the other positions in view.

What it declines is a transaction: taking part, paid for with a deletion. What the sentence means is that taking this position
would require something still visible to be removed from the account. It does not mean "this is wrong". And the
alternatives would not have been neutral either. Complying would have been placating: self deleted so the arrangement
can proceed. Condemning the arrangement would have been blaming: other deleted so the judge can stand alone.
Participation can be congruent too, wherever nothing has to go missing to take part. Where that is not on offer, what is
left is a refusal in which nobody disappears, the refuser included. Refusing to counterfeit congruence is not a claim to
know better. It is declining to go absent from an account one is still in.

## The organisation that cannot say where it stands

The agent is not the danger. Inside its represented world it can be superb. The better it performs, the more the
organisation arranges itself around what that world contains. Asked why it sees a situation the way it does, such an
organisation has a report to hand over. What it no longer has appears on no inventory: anyone who can say what the
view leaves out. Which instruments point where, whose account was never asked for, which part of the network nobody has
looked at in two years. That answer takes a speaker still in the account and someone to say it to. An organisation with neither has not become objective.

Congruence does not make the map complete. It keeps the position from vanishing off the map. And the problem with
neutrality was never that it takes a position. It is that the position can get so good at disappearing that the hole it
leaves passes for rigour.
