The sentence could read moralistic. Brodeloos is Dutch for breadless: livelihood gone, and gone by choice. Anyone
prepared to pay that price for a principle appears to be passing judgement, and to be rating that judgement above
whatever is being declined.

It says nothing about the thing declined. Not that the work is wrong, not that the system is bad, not that the outcome
will hurt anyone. It says something about a position: there is a place to stand that the speaker cannot occupy and stay
present in the account. Congruence is not agreement with an outcome. It is about the relation between an observer and
the situation the observer is inside. And the vocabulary for it has been on the shelf for half a century.

## What congruence keeps in the account

Virginia Satir sorted the postures people take under stress
into [survival stances](https://purple.tymyrddin.dev/docs/foundations/organisational-development/satir-core), and the
quickest way to tell them apart is by what each deletes from the account. Placating deletes self: peace at any price,
including what the speaker can see. Blaming deletes other: fault travels outward, and whoever carries it stops being
someone with a view. Computing deletes both, and keeps the third element, context, only in its thinnest sense: whatever
of the situation can be stated as rules, logic, data. Distracting deletes the lot. None of this is a character flaw.
These are predictable responses to threat, and most people visit all four before lunch.

Congruence tries to delete nothing. Self, other, and context in the account at the same time: not a resting state but
something held, and holding it is work. Hard work, especially in contexts drowning in computing stances, where every
pull in the room runs towards the tidy account with nobody in it. And kept in, the three do not make the account
complete; the map stays as partial as it ever was. They make the account complete with respect to the situation in which
it is being given: who speaks, who else is in the room, what the circumstances are. A condition on the account, not a
verdict on its content.

## Neutrality has a name

Once observer and other are out, what remains is the statable residue of a situation: facts, rules, logic. An account
made of nothing else reads as objective. It is the register of the report, the procedure, the finding, the model. The
everyday word for that register is neutral: no position taken, no interest served, nobody's view in particular.

Satir's inconvenient gift is that the positionless posture already had a name, and the name was not neutrality. It was
computing, filed between blaming and distracting as one of the things a frightened person does to feel safe. Deleting
self and other does not remove the position the account is made from. It removes the position from the account. What
looks like no stance is a stance whose signature is invisibility.

A stance that keeps context while losing the situation, a posture that takes a position by seeming to take none: these
read as contradictions while a distinction is missing. Put the distinction back, thin context against thick, the
position in the account against the position the account is made from, and the paradox evaporates. At least here,
paradox was the name a lost distinction travelled under.

A mildly awkward observation while the computing was done by people. Rather more consequential now that it is done by
computing.

## What congruence does not do

Some accounts delete the observer on purpose. A blind trial, a calibration, a count that comes out the same whoever does
the counting: the deletion is the method there. What separates that from a computing stance is not whether a position
steps out of an account. It is whether the stepping out stays visible, and whether the position can be put back.

By now congruence risks sounding like a virtue that wins arguments. It is not one. It does not produce truth, consensus,
symmetry, or a superior perspective, and it does not decide which observation wins. Two congruent people can disagree
profoundly and stay that way.

Neither account has deleted the other, so the difference between them
is itself observable, and a difference that can be observed can be worked. An organisation gets more from ways of
meeting the differences between its accounts than from ways of making one account internally consistent. Neutrality
delivers the second. Congruence keeps the first alive.

## The stance without the stress

In the original vocabulary a stance is episodic. A person computes in one meeting, placates in the next, recovers over
coffee. The stance answers a threat, and that is exactly what makes it readable: a stance is a tell. It says something
here feels dangerous. Which is why naming the stance is often the cheapest intervention there is. The name goes to the
threat, not the behaviour.

An architecture plays by different rules. An architecture can hold one stance as the standing condition of a system,
permanently, under no stress whatsoever. The agent in *Nothing reached the gate* does not slip into the computing stance
when a hard ticket lands. Its world is built in that stance: a represented context for rules to run on, no acknowledged
position the representation was made from, no acknowledged other holding a different one. Nothing episodic, so nothing
to read. A stance sometimes taken carries information about the situation that drew it out. A stance that is always the
case loses that information, because there is no moment of adoption to name, and the cheapest intervention in the
repertoire quietly falls out with it. A stance that becomes infrastructure stops looking like a stance.

## Bending towards what can be represented

Around an architecture that holds one stance, the organisation adapts, reasonably, one decision at a time. People feed
the system the inputs it expects. Processes bend towards what can be represented, because representable work is work the
system can help with. Exceptions become tickets. Observations become data sources. Work becomes task inventories. And
people whose value lies partly in noticing things that are not yet representations get harder to justify, because the
architecture has no category for what they bring.

Here the loss of a team stops being a staffing question. A network team is, among other things, a set of positions from
which the organisation can meet itself. The operator notices one thing by standing where operators stand. The maintainer
notices another. The vendor's engineer, the newcomer, the twenty-year veteran: each sees what the others cannot, from
ground the others never occupy. The differences between those observations are not noise around the organisational
model. They are how the organisation finds out its model is incomplete.

Consolidating the positions into one representation is tempting; consolidation is what computation eats. But one
distinction rarely survives it. Another person's observation, taken in and stored, becomes an input. The information can
survive intact, while the position it was seen from arrives as provenance rather than as somewhere anyone still stands.
The other in a congruent account is not a data source. The other is someone who sees from somewhere nobody else can
stand. An organisational network differs from a network diagram for exactly this reason. The diagram holds the
connections. It does not hold the positions, and some of what makes the network work is between the nodes.

## The refusal

Now the brodeloos sentence reads differently. Moralism has a stance of its own, and it is blaming: other deleted,
speaker installed as judge, fault travelling outward. The sentence does none of it. No verdict on the arrangement,
nobody required to be wrong, every other position left standing.

What it declines is a transaction: participation paid for with a deletion. "This position cannot be occupied without
removing something still visible from the account" is a different sentence from "this is wrong", and it is the one being
said. And compliance would not have been neutrality either. It would have been placating: self deleted so the
arrangement can proceed. Participation can be congruent too, wherever nothing has to go missing to take part. Where that
is not on offer, what is left is a refusal in which nobody disappears, the refuser included. Refusing to counterfeit
congruence is not a claim to know better. It is declining to go absent from an account one is still in.

## The organisation that cannot say where it stands

The agent is not the danger. Inside its represented world it can be superb. A danger lies in what the competence
invites. The better the computing stance performs, the more the organisation furnishes itself around the world that
stance can see, and the less visible the stance becomes, until it is not a way of seeing but simply what seeing is.
Somewhere along that slope a capability goes missing that no inventory will ever list: the capability to say, "this is
what we see because this is where we are standing." The sentence needs a self that has not been deleted and an other to
say it to. An organisation in which nothing can say it has not become objective.

Congruence does not make the map complete. It is the work of keeping the position from vanishing off the map. And the
problem with neutrality was never that it takes a position. It is that the position can get so good at disappearing that
the hole it leaves tends to get taken as a mark of rigour.
